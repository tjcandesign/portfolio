AI + Leadership

Efficiency For What?

By TJ Cichecki · April 2026

KPMG's latest quarterly AI pulse survey just came out, and the stat that jumped out at me more than anything else was that three out of four executives are openly admitting that their company's AI strategy is more for show than actual internal guidance. Once you sit with that for a second, the rest of the numbers in the survey start to make a lot more sense, because if most AI strategies are basically theater, then every other weird contradiction in the data has a pretty clean explanation.

CEOs are stressed about AI in a way I don't think anyone really anticipated a year ago, with a majority reporting real anxiety about leading their companies through this transition and a meaningful chunk of them genuinely worried they could lose their jobs if they don't pull it off. Employees, meanwhile, are quietly sabotaging the rollout, with close to a third of them admitting to it outright and Gen Z in particular checking out of the whole effort at a notably higher rate. Two-thirds of executives believe their company has already suffered a data leak because someone worked around the approved tools, and when you ask workers and executives whether AI should actually be trusted for business-critical decisions, the gap between them is enormous, with most executives saying yes and almost none of the workers agreeing.

All of it traces back to one thing nobody in leadership has really figured out how to answer, which is the question their employees are actually asking. What is this for?

The Gap Isn't A Skills Problem

The easy read on this data is that employees are just "behind" and need to catch up, and that does seem to be the read the C-suite is running with, with most executives talking about cultivating a new class of "AI-elite" workers and a majority already planning to lay off the people who can't or won't use the tools. But that framing completely misses what's actually happening on the ground, because the employees who are avoiding AI aren't doing it out of laziness or some reflexive fear of technology, they're doing the math on a much simpler question, which is that if they make themselves ten times more productive, what actually happens next?

And at almost every company I've ever seen, the answer to that question is depressingly predictable. The target moves, the quota adjusts, the extra capacity gets absorbed back into more output rather than returned to anyone as time, and the person on the receiving end of that arrangement has figured it out well before leadership has even started to notice.

So when most employees say they trust AI more than they trust their manager on certain work tasks, that isn't really a statement about the technology so much as it's what people say when they've stopped trusting the person in charge of them.

So What Are We Saving The Time For?

The question nobody in the C-suite seems willing to actually answer out loud is a pretty basic one, which is that if AI is making work faster, what are we doing with the time it's giving back? The default answer, the one baked into every quarterly earnings call, is just "more output," so more campaigns and more tickets and more revenue per headcount, because that's how the bonus math works for everyone above a certain altitude, and that's what the roadmap quietly optimizes for.

But there's another version of this, and it's the one I actually think matters, which is that the gain from AI could be measured in hours given back rather than extra output demanded. Four-day weeks, 32-hour schedules, the same work getting done with a lot less of the grind around it. And most of what AI is genuinely good at is the kind of work most people hate doing anyway, like summarizing meetings and cleaning up spreadsheets and drafting first passes nobody wanted to write and chasing down the hundred little logistics that eat up half of everyone's day, so automating that stuff doesn't really shrink the job, it just concentrates it around the parts that humans are actually good at and actually want to be doing.

If that was the pitch, if leadership was out there saying clearly that we're using AI so you can work less rather than so we can demand more, I don't think a third of employees would still be sitting there trying to quietly sabotage the rollout.

The Version I Actually Care About

In nonprofit and agency work specifically, the math on this comes out especially clean, because when a small team saves fifteen hours a week on work that used to take a full person, that doesn't really look like a layoff opportunity so much as it looks like more impact per donor dollar, more program delivery, and a shorter week for a team that's been running on fumes for five years. That's the version of this technology that's actually worth rallying people around, and nobody is ever going to rally around a tool that's just going to be used to squeeze more out of them.

It's A Storytelling Problem

On the surface the data looks like a strategy problem, because plenty of companies don't have a formal plan in place and most of the ones that do are calling it theatrical, but those numbers are really downstream of something earlier, which is that leadership has plans, what leadership doesn't have is an answer to what all of this is actually for beyond "we have to do it because everyone else is doing it." And employees can smell that from a mile away, because they've sat through enough transformation initiatives in their careers to recognize when they're being treated as the input into something rather than the beneficiary of it, and when AI then shows up with no clear story about how it improves their actual day and no defined answer to what all this new efficiency is going to buy them, they do exactly what you'd expect them to do, which is go through the motions in meetings, quietly route around the tools, and wait for the whole thing to blow over.

The leadership crisis in AI isn't really a skills gap so much as it's a storytelling gap, and the companies that figure out what AI is actually for and then mean it are going to end up with employees who run toward it instead of around it. Everybody else is going to keep pouring money into pilots that die quietly in the middle layers while their CEOs lose sleep wondering why the rollout isn't working.
