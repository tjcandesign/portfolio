Your AI Strategy Is Pointed at the Wrong Thing

By TJ Cichecki · April 2026

TLDR

The companies winning with AI aren't using it to do the same work faster. They're using it to grow. The difference isn't better tools — it's a fundamentally different strategy.

  • 75% of AI's economic value is captured by just 20% of companies
  • 2–3x more likely to use AI for new growth vs. efficiency
  • 20% EBITDA uplift among McKinsey's AI leaders
  • $3:$1 return for every dollar invested in AI

If you run an agency right now, there's a good chance everything you've done with AI is oriented around one idea: doing the work you already do, but faster or with fewer people. It's the most intuitive play when a powerful tool shows up. But the data coming out of the major consultancies is making a convincing case that this is the wrong approach entirely.

The companies pulling ahead aren't just more efficient. They're treating AI as a growth technology, not a productivity tool. The top fifth of companies using AI are two to three times more likely to say it helped them find growth opportunities they wouldn't have found otherwise, and almost three times as likely to say it helped them rethink their business model.

Efficiency is a race to the bottom

For agencies, this distinction is the whole game. The default AI strategy of compressing timelines and reducing headcount leads exactly where you'd expect: every other agency is doing it too, and the only differentiator left is price. The ones that pull away will be the ones using AI to offer capabilities that weren't possible at their size before.

There's a reason individual productivity hasn't translated to organizational value. Everyone has their own AI habits, their own prompting styles, their own workflows, and none of it connects. Worse, people aren't just doing their own thing — they're ignoring the protocols leadership put in place, or actively sabotaging them. Getting from individual productivity to organizational value requires building actual systems, not just giving people better tools.

"AI has made every individual 10x more productive, but no company has become 10x more valuable as a result."

— George Savolka, a16z

McKinsey's top themes for AI leaders

  • Technology alone doesn't create advantage — enduring capabilities do
  • Focus on economic leverage points, not general efficiency
  • Every AI transformation is a people transformation — 70%+ of AI talent should be in-house
  • Speed is the defining organizational advantage
  • Relearn like your business depends on it

What it looks like in practice

A good example of how I've seen this in practice is how Ramp (corporate cards & spend management) built their internal AI system. Nearly all of their employees use AI daily. Every new employee gets a configured workspace on day one with the company's tools and context already connected — that's how institutional knowledge builds from the start. When someone builds a better workflow, that skill gets shared with the entire team. They have hundreds of shared skills and an AI marketplace that lets employees discover the best ones based on their role and context.

Ramp's three principles

  • Don't limit anyone's upside. Make complexity invisible while preserving full capability.
  • One person's breakthrough becomes everyone's baseline. When someone figures out a workflow, share it.
  • The product is the enablement. People learn by doing, not by attending workshops.

One interesting fact is that they deliberately rejected the idea of simplifying AI for non-technical users. Everyone can become empowered when you pair AI's ability to help people work through problems with the simplicity of the tools. The tools help us build and help us learn.

What this means for agencies

You don't need the same technology as a venture-backed Silicon Valley startup. You need principles you can scale down to match your size agency. Owners should help their staff connect their tools with all the context their employees have, so AI has real information about your clients and projects instead of starting from zero every time. When someone on your team figures out an AI workflow that works, it should be shared as an asset among the company instead of living in one person's head.

And most importantly: stop thinking of AI as the thing that makes you faster at the old work. Start thinking about what new work it makes possible. The agencies that treat AI as an internal operating system, one that compounds as the team learns from each other, are going to end up somewhere completely different than the ones still using it to shave hours off the same timeline.
