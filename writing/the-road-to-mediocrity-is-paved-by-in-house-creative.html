Creative Leadership

The Road to Mediocrity Is Paved by In-House Creative

By TJ Cichecki · Published May 8, 2026

Frozen pizza put a lot of pizzerias out of business. The bar just moved. If you couldn't beat the thing already sitting in my freezer, why did I need you at all? Seth Godin made that point recently and it stuck with me, because that freezer is pretty much what AI has become for the creative business. Good enough is sitting right there now, instant, free, and infinitely scalable, which is more or less the same thing I was getting at when I wrote that the floor of acceptable visual output has risen and the value of passable has dropped with it. So the question every creative team should be asking itself is dead simple: why would anyone leave the house for what we make?

A lot of teams are going to fail that test, and most won't see it coming, because they've spent years getting really good at the wrong thing.

What happens inside the room

Here's what happens inside a lot of in-house creative teams. The work stops being about the work. Politics start running it instead, and the unspoken rule is that nobody rocks the boat too hard. Nobody in the room actually knows what a great idea looks like, so the second one person nods at something, everybody piles on, and just like that mediocre becomes the consensus. The one person who can tell it's weak is too nervous to say so, because pushing back feels like a career risk. And the final call ends up getting made by an account manager who has never once sat in the chair for ten straight hours actually building the thing.

That last part is the one that gets me.

I'm not saying account people shouldn't have opinions. They should. Diversity of opinion is exactly what makes a concept better, and there are a hundred real decisions buried in any piece of work, font, spacing, placement, hierarchy, all of it, where a fresh set of eyes can catch something the maker missed. If you've got a reason and you can back it up, bring it, every time. But if your note is just "I don't love it" or "something feels off," with nothing behind it but a gut feeling, then I'm going to trust the trained creative who's lived in this craft for years over the gut of someone in an adjacent role. That isn't ego. It's the same reason you don't let me run your client relationships. We each have a job here. Creative direction is the creative team's responsibility, and everything around the creative work, the client relationship and the broader business experience, is what account management is actually trained for. Steering the creative itself isn't part of that.

Realistic is how "fine" wins

There's an old line that being realistic is the most commonly traveled road to mediocrity, and it's been on my mind ever since AI started flooding the market with passable work. Realistic is how "fine" wins. You see it all over the place, government work, DC trade associations, big slow organizations where fine technically does the job and nobody pushes for more. But if fine is the goal, then be honest about it and ask yourself why you're paying for designers at all. Hand it to your account managers, let them run it through whatever AI tool they're learning this week, and you'll get exactly what you asked for. Mediocre, on time, on budget. That's already starting to happen, and it's going to flood the market with work that looks like everyone else's, because that's what these tools do when nobody with real taste is steering them.

Which is the whole point. Good enough used to be a floor that took some real effort to clear, and now it's just free, so the only thing left worth paying for is work that's actually great, the campaign that does something nobody expected and moves a real number in your business.

What outside teams do differently

So if that's what you want, hire for it. Hire an outside team that doesn't carry your politics around with them. We don't know that Susan took too much sick leave last quarter and we don't care, because none of that has anything to do with solving your problem. The way I work is simple. I take my client's problems and I make them my projects, and then I finish those projects and deliver solutions that actually solve the thing. That's literally the job. People in-house do what the manager asked for and go home. We have to earn it every single day, sell the work every single day, to people who are paying attention. That's the difference, and on our best day there's nobody better at it.

There's never been a worse time to be mediocre, and there's never been a better moment to do work that's worth leaving the house for.
